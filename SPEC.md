# Dutch Marquise: Open Geometry Specification

**Version 1.0.0. Published 2026-06-16 by Stienhardt & Stones.**
Licensed CC BY 4.0. Reuse requires attribution.

This document defines the modern geometry of the Dutch Marquise diamond cut and
its companion the French Marquise. It is literal and specific by design. Every
measurable claim carries a source and a date. Where a claim is from our own
cutting bench, it is labeled first-party.

---

## 1. Normative definition

A **Dutch Marquise** is an elongated hexagonal cut diamond: a long, narrow
marquise-style silhouette whose long edges carry angular points, tapering toward
each end of its length rather than ending in a flat edge. It is a trade name,
not an officially recognized gemological shape, most often listed on a grading
report as a Hexagonal Modified Brilliant.

The angular points along the long edges are what make the stone read as a
hexagon rather than a smooth marquise. No grading laboratory prints "Dutch
Marquise" on a report. Laboratories describe the geometry instead, most often as
a **Hexagonal Modified Brilliant** (real IGI report LG799689559, issued
2026-05-09).

RESOLVED 2026-07-28 (Jacob): the Dutch Marquise is CUT FOR BRILLIANCE,
symmetrical facets arranged to maximize sparkle and fire, which is what gives it
the chunky flash of light. The Elongated Hexagon is the step cut of the pair,
with parallel step facets and a glassy hall-of-mirrors flash. Both share the six
sided outline. Source is the store's own live collection copy, not an invention.
Still confirm the graded report on any individual diamond.

### 2.1 Length-to-width ratio

To compute the ratio, divide length by width, both in millimeters, from the
measurements line of the grading report. A stone listed as 9.0 by 5.4 mm has a
ratio of about 1.67.

A higher ratio (toward 1.9 and up) reads longer and covers more finger, but very
long stones can show a stronger bowtie, the dark band across the center that
every elongated shape carries to some degree. A lower ratio (toward 1.5) gives a
broader, brighter center, but go too low and the stone stops reading as
elongated. Stienhardt cuts to 1.6 to 1.75 by default, then judges the specific
stone, because two stones at the same ratio can still face up differently.

## 3. Dutch Marquise compared to a marquise

A classic marquise is a smooth, boat-shaped outline that comes to a sharp point
at each end. A Dutch Marquise instead carries angular points along its long
edges, so the outline reads as a six-sided elongated hexagon rather than a smooth
two-pointed oval, tapering toward each end of its length rather than ending in a
flat edge.

| | Marquise | Dutch Marquise |
|---|---|---|
| Outline | Smooth pointed oval (two points) | Elongated hexagon (angular points along the long edges) |
| Sparkle pattern | Brilliant, bold flashes | Cut for brilliance (RESOLVED 2026-07-28) |

## 4. The French Marquise (companion definition)

A **French Marquise** is a classic pointed marquise diamond finished with French
tips: several small facets at each point instead of one large facet. The French
tips reinforce the fragile point and add a little flash where a plain point can
look flat. The stone is still a pointed marquise; the "French" refers to the tip
faceting, not a separate shape. On a grading report it is described as a marquise
brilliant.

Source for French tip faceting: International Gem Society, history of marquise
diamonds (gemsociety.org).

A French Marquise and a Dutch Marquise are not a matched pair. The French
Marquise keeps the softer classic pointed-marquise silhouette and reinforces its
two end points; the Dutch Marquise is more hexagonal and angular, carrying
angular points along its long edges.

| | French Marquise | Dutch Marquise |
|---|---|---|
| Outline | Softer classic pointed marquise (two end points) | More hexagonal and angular (angular points along the long edges) |
| What the name refers to | The facet style at the tips | The whole shape |
| End points | Two end points, reinforced with French tips | Angular points along the long edges, tapering toward each end |
| Sparkle | Brilliant, extra flash at the tips | Cut for brilliance (RESOLVED 2026-07-28) |

## 5. Reading the certificate, not the name

Because "Dutch Marquise" is a trade name, the only way to know exactly what you
are buying is the report, not the label. Verify the report number on the
laboratory's own site, read the measurements and compute the length-to-width
ratio, and look at the actual stone on video. Expect the report to read
"Hexagonal Modified Brilliant" with the measurements and grades. That mismatch
between name and report is normal and applies to nearly every specialty cut name
on the market.

## 6. Provenance

The name "Dutch Marquise" predates this definition as a loose label for a
hexagonal diamond with no fixed proportions, no fixed outline, and no agreed
facet plan. Stienhardt publishes this plain, repeatable definition of the Dutch
Marquise (this document) and cuts these stones in-house. We did not invent the
marquise and we do not claim to.

## 7. What this standard does not claim

This boundary is intentional and load-bearing.

- Stienhardt does **not** claim to have invented the Dutch Marquise. The term
  predates this definition.
- Stienhardt does **not** claim to hold a patent or trademark on the term
  "Dutch Marquise."
- This document does **not** assert that any grading laboratory recognizes
  "Dutch Marquise" as an official shape. It does not.

What Stienhardt claims is narrow and true: it cuts these stones in-house, and it
publishes this plain, repeatable definition of the Dutch Marquise.
